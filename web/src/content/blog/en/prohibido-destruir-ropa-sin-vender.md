In 2018, a well-known British luxury house disclosed in its annual report that it had destroyed £28.6 million worth of goods in a single year. The story went around the world and the brand announced it would stop. Eight years on, what was then a scandal is, in Europe, an offence.

## What the rule says

The **Ecodesign for Sustainable Products Regulation (EU) 2024/1781** (known as ESPR) bans the destruction of unsold consumer products. For **clothing, clothing accessories and footwear**, the ban has applied since **19 July 2026**.

- **Large companies:** covered since 19 July 2026.
- **Medium-sized companies:** from 19 July 2030.
- **Micro and small companies:** exempt for now.

Companies that are covered must also **report every year** how many unsold products they discard, how much they weigh and why. The European Commission adopted a common reporting format in February 2026, which applies from 2027.

There are narrow exceptions, for instance on health or safety grounds, or when a product is damaged beyond repair. But the general rule is clear: what doesn't sell must be donated, resold, repaired or recycled, not destroyed.

## Why it matters even if you're small

If your brand has only a few shops, the ban doesn't bind you today. It affects you anyway, for three reasons:

1. **Surplus still costs money.** Not being allowed to destroy it doesn't make it disappear. Storing it, marking it down or selling it to an outlet has a cost, and that cost is lost margin.
2. **The rule arrives in steps.** What applies to large companies today applies to medium-sized ones in 2030. Growing brands run into the obligation along the way.
3. **Your customers are asking.** What happens to unsold clothes is no longer an expert topic. A brand that can explain how much it has left over, and why it's less each season, has a story to tell.

## The best surplus is the one you never make

The rule tackles the end of the problem: what to do with leftovers. But leftovers are decided much earlier, when the order is placed. Brands overproduce because they can't tell precisely what will work, in which size and in which shop.

The till helps, but it's late: it only counts what has already sold. What signals surplus in advance is something else: **garments that get tried on a lot and sell little**. That garment attracts interest, but something goes wrong in the fitting room (the size, the cut, the price). If you know in week three of the season, you can fix the next order. If you find out in January, it's already surplus.
