Turn the T-shirt you're wearing inside out and find the label. You'll probably read something like "100% cotton" and, if you're lucky, "Made in" followed by a country. It looks like plenty of information. In fact, it's very little.

## The only requirement: fibres

In the European Union, textile labels are governed by **Regulation (EU) 1007/2011** on textile fibre names and labelling. What it requires is essentially one thing: **stating which fibres the garment is made of, and in what proportion**, using harmonised names ("cotton", "polyester", "linen"…), on a physical label that is durable and legible.

The familiar washing instructions are not required by this regulation: they are a voluntary symbol system, even though almost every brand uses it.

## Country of manufacture is optional

This surprises almost everyone: **EU rules do not require clothing to show its country of manufacture**. A brand can include it or not. Some countries have their own rules for online sales (France, for instance, now requires the place of manufacture to be shown near the price), but in general "Made in" is the brand's choice.

## And when it appears, it only tells the ending

When "Made in" does appear, it usually refers to the country where the garment underwent its **last substantial transformation**: nearly always, the sewing. But a garment passes through many hands before that:

1. **Fibre:** where the cotton was grown or the polyester made.
2. **Spinning:** where the fibre became yarn.
3. **Weaving or knitting:** where the fabric was made.
4. **Dyeing and finishing:** where it got its colour and feel.
5. **Making:** where it was cut and sewn.

A T-shirt "Made in Portugal" may contain cotton from India, spun in Turkey and dyed somewhere else. The label only tells the last line of a five-chapter story.

## What's coming

The European Commission is reviewing the textile labelling regulation, and one idea under study is to allow **digital information** alongside the physical label. At the same time, the upcoming **digital product passport** will require structured information about each garment, reachable from the product itself. The composition label isn't going away, but it will no longer be the only place where a garment says what it is.

## How to read a label today

In the meantime, three useful questions when you shop:

- **Does it name the country?** If not, ask. A brand that knows is usually happy to tell you.
- **Which stage is it?** "Made in" is almost always the sewing. What about the fabric? The fibre?
- **Where does the information come from?** Is it declared by the brand, documented by the supplier or certified by someone?
